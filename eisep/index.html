Dale's Principle as an Inductive Bias in RNNs

Real neurons are either excitatory or inhibitory, never both. Artificial networks ignore this, and earlier attempts to impose it made training unstable. EISep is a recurrent network that enforces the rule through a learnable excitatory–inhibitory separation, and in these experiments it trains more stably, develops sparse and modular connectivity, and does better on continual learning and reinforcement learning than a vanilla RNN.

The idea: one sign per neuron

Dale's principle says each biological neuron is either excitatory or inhibitory: it releases the same type of neurotransmitter at all of its synapses, so every connection it sends out has the same sign. Artificial networks work differently. A unit can send positive weights to some neurons and negative weights to others, which makes optimization easy and biology unrecognizable.

Imposing the rule has a reputation for hurting. Earlier work suggests it can reduce capacity, narrow the loss basin and impair learning, so most networks keep their unconstrained units. This project asks whether Dale's principle can instead work as a useful inductive bias, a built-in assumption that steers learning toward good solutions.

The catch is that splitting a mixed-sign neuron into an excitatory one and an inhibitory one could reduce expressivity at the same hidden size. Earlier attempts to fix every neuron's identity narrowed the solution space and made optimization harder, which led to degraded performance and slower or unstable training.

EISep makes two changes to avoid that, covered in the method: the sign of each neuron is learned, and the weights start from a distribution that keeps the initial spectral radius small.

  • Initialization

The method: EISep

Starting point

A standard RNN with hidden size N, activation φ, recurrent weights W, input weights U and output weights V:

h_t = phi( W h_{t-1} + U x_t + b_U + b_W )
o_t = V h_t + b_V

Simply starting neurons as excitatory or inhibitory isn't enough, because unconstrained optimization flips the signs over time. The usual fix is W = QD, where Q is a learnable non-negative matrix and D is a fixed diagonal matrix of ±1, with non-negativity enforced by gradient masking or by a non-negative transform.

Learnable signs with Gumbel-Softmax

EISep replaces the fixed D with a learned one, using the Gumbel-Softmax reparameterization so the sign choice stays differentiable. 

M      in R^{N x 2}                  learnable logits, one row per neuron
m_i    = GSoftmax(M_i)               soft sign mask for neuron i
e_i    = m_i[0] - m_i[1]             soft sign value, +1 or -1 in most cases
D      = diag(e_1, ..., e_N)

W_0    in R^{N x N}, W_0,ij ~ N(0, 1/N)
W      = ReLU(W_0) D                 non-negative values, one sign per neuron

test time:  e_i = +1 if M_i[0] > M_i[1] else -1       (no sampling noise)

L      = L_task + lambda_e * L_e
L_e    = (1/N) * sum_i H( softmax(M_i) )                  entropy of each neuron's sign
  • Learnable sign matrix. Neurons can explore different E/I organizations and follow more flexible optimization trajectories.
  • Rectified Gaussian initialization. ReLU(W_0) starts from a rectified Gaussian, which controls the initial spectral radius better than the exponential or gamma samples used before.
  • Entropy regularization. The term L_e pushes each neuron's sign toward a firm choice, so identities are stable later in training. In the paper's runs it drops below 10−2 within the first quarter of training, long before the task loss converges, so signs settle early.
  • Backbones. The same idea is applied to LSTM and GRU recurrent matrices for harder tasks, with one sign mask shared across all the gates so each neuron keeps one identity.

Baselines

EISep is compared with the vanilla RNN (called Simple) and three Dale-constrained designs. The original ColEI and DANN use plain SGD, while these experiments use Adam, so Adam variants of both were added for a fair comparison.

SimpleVanilla RNN with a Gaussian initialization and unconstrained weights.
ColEIExplicit value-sign split W = QD with a fixed E/I ratio. Weights are sampled from exponential distributions and rescaled to a spectral radius of 1.5, and Q is clipped to be non-negative after every update. Run as ColEI-SGD and ColEI-Adam.
DBPNDale's backpropagation. Dale's principle is enforced by projecting weights back to the correct sign after updates, with a uniform initialization and fixed identities.
DANNSeparate excitatory and inhibitory pathways, W = WEE − WEIWIE, with three non-negative matrices and exponential initialization. Run as DANN-SGD and DANN-Adam.

Multi-task cognitive benchmark

The first test is a benchmark of 20 interrelated cognitive tasks, widely used to study multi-task capacity in RNNs. One network has to switch between task rules from trial to trial.

Each trial gives the network a rule input saying which task is active, then noisy sensory stimuli. The correct choice and stimulus strength are randomized, so easy and hard decisions are mixed. The suite tests working memory, perceptual decision-making and rule-based context switching in a single network.

Results

So the headline result here is parity, not a win: enforcing Dale's principle doesn't have to cost task performance, and EISep reaches Simple's accuracy where earlier Dale-constrained models fall well short.

Training stability

Most baselines show occasional spikes in loss and gradient norm. EISep's curves are lower and steadier, and it holds the spectral radius of the recurrent matrix tighter than most of them. Across learning rates the pattern is clear: Simple's gradient norm blows up at a learning rate of 0.05, rising to roughly 1012, while EISep's stays in range, and at the highest learning rate tried (0.5) EISep still scores about 0.5 while Simple falls to about 0.1.

EISep also keeps a tighter recurrent balance, defined as the average input to each neuron. In biological networks, balance between excitation and inhibition is known to stabilize dynamics, and the same mechanism appears to emerge here.

Training setup
  • All recurrent networks use 64 hidden units and are trained with backpropagation through time.
  • Softplus activation, Adam optimizer, cross-entropy loss, batch size 32, sequence length 100, 3000 epochs.
  • Gumbel-Softmax temperature 0.1, with no annealing. Entropy-loss coefficient λe = 1.
  • Input and output weights use orthogonal initialization. The E–I logit matrix starts at N(0, 1/N).
  • 10 random seeds for the main comparison, and 5 seeds for the learning-rate sweep over 10−4 to 0.5 (8 values). Error bars are standard errors across seeds.
  • Experiments ran on a single consumer laptop GPU (RTX 4060, 8 GB). About 12 minutes per multi-task run, and tens of GPU-hours in total including tuning and ablations.

Structure that emerges: modular and sparse

Two more properties of real neural circuits appear in EISep without being built in: functional modules and sparse connectivity.

Modularity, seen on a two-choice task

On a noisy two-alternative forced-choice task, the network must pick the stronger of two noisy signals. A signal-detection index d′ sorts the neurons into Group 1 (selective for signal 1), Group 2 (selective for signal 2) and non-selective neurons.

Modularity and sparsity, measured

To measure modularity across the whole 20-task benchmark, neurons are partitioned with two spectral clustering methods suited to directed, signed graphs (directed and lifted), and the partitions are scored with signed modularity, which rewards positive weights inside modules and negative weights between them. EISep has the highest modularity under both methods.

Sparsity is the fraction of small weights (|w| < 0.1σ). EISep reaches 0.87 against about 0.05 to 0.06 for Simple and DANN, and 0.26 to 0.34 for ColEI and DBPN. Zeroing out those small weights leaves performance essentially unchanged, which supports using the measure.

Continual learning

When tasks arrive one after another, their gradients interfere and the network forgets earlier tasks. A modular network can reduce this by giving tasks different subspaces, which is what EISep's structure suggests.

Setup: four tasks in a 2×2 design

No delayDelay
Respond to the stronger channel (Go)GoDelay Go
Respond to the weaker channel (Anti)AntiDelay Anti

The design separates the sensory-response mapping (Go vs. Anti) from the memory requirement (delay vs. no delay). Each task is trained for 200 epochs in the order Go, Anti, Delay Go, Delay Anti, and the full round is repeated 10 times, giving 40 task trials. The task rule is given on every trial, and performance on all four tasks is tested after each one. Each model runs at its best learning rate from the multi-task sweep (Simple 0.005, EISep 0.01), with 5 seeds.

At the final round, averaged over the four last trials, EISep scores 0.96 against Simple's 0.55. Only DANN-Adam (0.90) comes close. The loss curves tell the same story: EISep's decoding loss is lower with far fewer spikes.

Simple's loss blows up several times, reaching around 1026 at a learning rate of 0.01, which is the instability behind its forgetting.

Less gradient interference

Cosine similarity between the average gradients for each task shows how the tasks interact. EISep has high similarity for tasks with the same stimulus-response mapping (Go and Delay Go, Anti and Delay Anti) and near-zero similarity for opposing tasks (Go and Anti). Simple shows comparable similarity for both kinds. Similar tasks share a gradient direction in EISep, and conflicting tasks are kept in separate directions.

The cumulative weight updates show structure too: positively correlated for same-type tasks and negatively correlated for opposing ones in EISep, with no such pattern in Simple. The paper notes that negative update correlations can sit alongside near-zero gradient similarity, since cumulative updates also depend on optimizer dynamics and training history.

A weight-transfer test

To probe the geometry of the updates, the Delay Go→Delay Anti update is normalized, averaged over trials and added to the weights after training on Go, W_Go + α·ΔW. As α grows, EISep's performance moves from Go to Anti, and Simple's doesn't. The paper reads this as EISep's weights lying in a structured, low-dimensional subspace that allows parameter reuse and less interference, while Simple's weights are more entangled.

The main experiments use an alternating order (Go, Anti, Delay Go, Delay Anti). A grouped order (Go, Delay Go, Anti, Delay Anti) is harder for every model, and EISep falls from 0.96 to 0.75 but keeps its edge over Simple.

The explanation offered is gradient interference: two similar tasks in a row push the network into a narrow subspace, which makes the next, opposing task harder to learn. Alternating interleaves the conflicting tasks.

More continual-learning curves

Reinforcement learning

Reinforcement learning is known for unstable optimization and high-variance gradients, which are the problems EISep's stable dynamics should help with.

Setup

The agents use Advantage Actor-Critic (A2C), with the actor and critic as separate recurrent modules. That allows four variants, depending on where EISep is used:

Actor: SimpleActor: EISep
Critic: SimpleSimpleEISep(A)
Critic: EISepEISep(C)EISep(A+C)
  • Lunar Lander (LunarLander-v3): a discrete control task with 4 actions. Episodes are capped at 500 steps.
  • HalfCheetah (HalfCheetah-v5): a harder continuous control task. The backbone is upgraded from a vanilla RNN to an LSTM to speed up training.

Results

Every variant with an EISep critic clearly beats Simple on both tasks. An EISep actor alone doesn't help on Lunar Lander (10.1 against 19.9) and helps only modestly on HalfCheetah (2020 against 1687), so on these tasks most of the gain comes from the critic. EISep(A+C) is best on Lunar Lander (133.9 against 19.9, with chance at about −200) and EISep(C) is best on HalfCheetah (4332 against 1687). Other Dale-constrained baselines also do worse than EISep on Lunar Lander. EISep also helps a plain feedforward network, though by less than in the recurrent case, which suggests it eases gradient instability that comes from the RL task itself as well as from recurrence.

Inside the network: smoother, more informative dynamics

To see what E–I separation does to the agent's internal state, the hidden activity of the actor is plotted over a short clip of a Lunar Lander test episode.

How informative is the internal state about what the agent is doing? A Hidden Markov Model (5 latent states) is fitted to the hidden trajectories without supervision. Separately, each Lunar Lander state is labelled with a phase by fixed rules on the environment variables, such as free fall, touchdown preparation and landing, without looking at the agent's internals. The normalized mutual information between the two labellings measures how much of the environment the dynamics encode.

EISep is much higher than Simple for both actor and critic. The Simple critic's states have collapsed and carry almost no information about the environment, and the Simple agent tends to hover near the ground and rarely reaches well-defined landing phases.

Three more HMM measures agree. EISep's latent states are stickier (higher self-transition probability), its actor has lower transition entropy, and both of its networks switch states far less often, all consistent with smoother internal dynamics.

The ablations barely matter on the simplest benchmark and matter a lot on the hardest. On multi-task learning Abs-EISep, Fixed-EISep, EntryEI and EISep(N1) stay within a few points of EISep, and Fixed-EISep even ties it, so E–I separation alone is enough for relatively simple tasks. Only EISep(N2) (0.82) and Cstr-Simple (0.86) fall further behind. On continual and reinforcement learning every ablation falls behind, in places severely.

  • Learnable signs matter. Fixed-EISep drops to 0.51 on continual learning and 56.8 on Lunar Lander. A variant that starts from the signs of a trained EISep but reinitializes everything else does no better than fixed signs in RL, so what helps is adapting signs throughout training, not only finding a good starting configuration. Freezing signs partway through training also hurts continual learning and RL.
  • The ReLU matters. Swapping it for an absolute value removes the sparsity advantage, lets the spectral radius grow without bound, and collapses continual learning to 0.00.
  • Neuron-level separation beats a more flexible alternative. EntryEI has more freedom but underperforms EISep on every task, so the biologically realistic constraint is better than the larger hypothesis space.
  • Initialization alone is not the story. Cstr-Simple, a vanilla RNN with EISep's initialization, reaches only 0.12 on continual learning and 26.6 on Lunar Lander.
  • It is not just regularization. L1 regularization on Simple can reach EISep's level of sparsity on the supervised settings, but performance stays worse in continual learning and becomes unstable in multi-task learning. In RL, even extremely large L1 coefficients fail to reach comparable sparsity and give worse performance.

Sensitivity

  • Hidden size. EISep stays robust as the network grows, while other models overfit and degrade, so the gap to Simple widens with size.
  • Gumbel-Softmax temperature. A very low temperature approaches frozen signs and costs a little. A high one lets noise dominate, flipping signs and breaking the train-test match, which badly hurts multi-task and continual learning.
  • Entropy coefficient. Without the entropy loss (λe = 0), signs keep occasionally flipping and performance drops significantly.
  • Activation. Switching Softplus to ReLU barely affects multi-task learning, but RL training becomes unstable from repeated gradient explosions.

Limits and honest caveats

  • Slower at low learning rates. EISep does worse than Simple when the learning rate is small, so there is a trade-off between stability and convergence speed. Adaptive methods to widen its stable range are future work.
  • The benefit depends on the task. On supervised benchmarks where gradient instability isn't the main problem (the adding problem, sequential MNIST, a sorting task), EISep still beats most Dale-constrained models but its edge over Simple is much weaker. That fits the no-free-lunch idea: no inductive bias is best everywhere, and this one pays off where optimization is non-stationary or high-variance.
  • Scale is untested. Models here have 64 hidden units, and larger sizes up to 256 look good, but it is unknown whether the advantage holds in much larger models or harder problems.
  • It is not a faithful brain model. The excitatory-to-inhibitory ratio is typically around 4:1 in the brain, and long-range projections are mostly excitatory, and neither is reproduced. The aim is to learn why biological systems work, not to copy them.
  • Learnable signs are a deliberate bend of biology. A neuron changing type seems unbiological, but training also stands in for developmental and evolutionary change. The signs settle early in training, in the first quarter, which is consistent with neuronal differentiation.
