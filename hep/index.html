Dark Matter Collider Simulation

A chained simulation workflow that takes a custom dark matter model, written as a Lagrangian with dark matter equations, all the way to simulated detector data and analysis. The theory is defined in Mathematica, the collision is generated in MadGraph and Pythia, the detector response comes from GEANT4, and the results are analysed in the EiC simulator.

What it is

High-energy physics (HEP) studies the smallest building blocks of matter by smashing particles together at enormous energies and looking at what comes out. This project studies dark matter particle collisions: collisions in which a hypothetical dark matter particle could be produced. Dark matter is thought to make up a significant portion of the universe, yet it remains largely undetected.

The model behind the collisions is a Lagrangian, a single mathematical expression that encodes every particle in a theory, its properties and how the particles interact. Here it is a custom Lagrangian that includes dark matter equations and the new dark particles. The project's job is to predict what those particles would look like when they fly out of a collision, and what a detector would record if they did.

No single program does all of that, so the project is a pipeline: five tools chained together, where the output of each becomes the input of the next. Change the theory at the start and its effect can be followed through to detector-level results without redoing the plumbing by hand.

Who did what. The physics model itself was specified by a PhD student collaborator. My contribution was the software and integration side: building the custom model in Mathematica, getting it into MadGraph, connecting every tool into one end-to-end pipeline, and working with the detector geometry in GEANT4. Details are in My role.

The idea, with watches

Imagine smashing two watches together at high speed. You don't care about the watches themselves. You care about the tiny gears, springs and screws that fly out, because those reveal how a watch is built on the inside. Now imagine one of those watches might contain a part nobody has ever seen before. The job is to predict what that mystery part would look like when it flies out, and what a detector would record if it did.

The five tools

What each tool is, what it does in particle physics, and which part of the collision it handles.

1. Mathematica the theory

What it is. A general-purpose symbolic computation system from Wolfram. It can manipulate equations algebraically, not just crunch numbers, which makes it a standard tool for writing down theoretical physics models.

What it does here. Physicists describe a theory with a Lagrangian. In Mathematica, packages such as FeynRules turn a Lagrangian into the interaction rules (Feynman rules) that simulators need, and export them in a standard model format that event generators like MadGraph can read.

Part of the collision it handles. None directly. Mathematica does not simulate collisions. It defines the physics that the later tools simulate.

2. MadGraph the impact

What it is. MadGraph (MadGraph5_aMC@NLO) is a Monte Carlo event generator that calculates what happens in the core particle interaction.

What it does. Given a model and a collision process (for example, “two protons collide and produce dark matter particles”), it generates all the possible Feynman diagrams for that process, calculates the probability (cross section) of each outcome, and produces simulated collision events by randomly sampling those probabilities.

Part of the collision it handles. The hard scatter, the initial, highest-energy moment where the fundamental particles interact and the primary particles are produced. This is “part 1” of the collision. Its output is a list of freshly created particles, typically in the Les Houches Event (LHE) file format.

3. Pythia the debris

What it is. A Monte Carlo event generator specialized in everything that happens after the hard scatter.

  • Parton showering. Particles radiate and split into cascades of more particles.
  • Hadronization. Quarks and gluons cannot exist alone, so they bind together into composite particles (hadrons) such as protons, pions and kaons.
  • Decays. Unstable particles decay into lighter ones until only long-lived, detectable particles remain.
  • Underlying event. The “leftover” interactions from the rest of the colliding particles.

Part of the collision it handles. Everything from right after the hard scatter to the final set of particles that would leave the collision point. It picks up exactly where MadGraph leaves off.

4. GEANT4 the detector

What it is. A C++ toolkit developed at CERN for simulating how particles travel through and interact with matter.

What it does. You describe a detector, including its geometry, shapes, positions and materials, and GEANT4 tracks every particle through it. It models the physical processes along the way: ionization, scattering, energy loss, particle showers and so on. The result is a record of where and how much energy was deposited in each part of the detector. Here the geometry is custom, including components such as argon chambers with lead walls.

Part of the collision it handles. None of the collision itself. GEANT4 handles the detector response: what a real instrument would actually see of the particles that Pythia produced.

5. EiC Simulator the analysis

What it is. The simulation and analysis software for the Electron-Ion Collider (EIC), a next-generation collider facility. The software is written in C++, typically on top of the ROOT framework. On the project poster the software environment is called EIC-shell.

What it does. It takes the simulated detector-level data and runs the analysis: reconstructing what happened in each event, applying detector effects and computing the physical quantities and distributions of interest.

Why it's used here. Analysis means processing very large numbers of events. Because the software is compiled C++ rather than an interpreted language, it is much faster for this kind of heavy number crunching.

Part of the collision it handles. None directly. It works on the end products of the whole chain and turns raw simulated data into results.

How the pipeline was used

The tools were chained in order, with the output of each becoming the input of the next.

  1. Mathematica produced a custom Lagrangian model that included dark matter equations and the new dark particles. The model was exported in a format MadGraph can read.
  2. MadGraph loaded that custom model and simulated the hard scatter, producing events containing the new dark particles.
  3. Pythia took MadGraph's output events and simulated the later stages: showering, hadronization and decays.
  4. GEANT4 received the resulting final-state particles and passed them through a custom-built detector geometry, including components such as argon chambers with lead walls.
  5. EiC Simulator took the results from the preceding stages and ran the analysis, benefiting from its optimized C++ performance.

The project poster

The project was presented as a poster at a student design showcase. It frames the pipeline as a single software tool, with a GUI on top, for studying dark matter collisions.

Objective

Create a single software tool that lets users study high-energy dark matter particle collisions through detectors, using a dark matter Lagrangian model.

Features

  • A single environment that pipes the physics software together: Mathematica, MadGraph, Pythia and EIC-shell.
  • A custom-made dark matter Lagrangian model.
  • Dark matter Feynman diagrams.
  • A script template for users to define their own models.
  • Dark particles and interactions.
  • A GUI for generating and studying collisions with button clicks, which lowers the technical literacy needed to study collisions on existing models.

The poster's pipeline diagram also lists the outputs the tool produces: the collision sub-processes, the Feynman diagrams and the collision information.

Checking the model. According to the poster, a post-doc working in high-energy physics took an interest in the project and recreated the same particle collisions independently, to help verify that the model was working correctly.
